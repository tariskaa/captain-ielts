Part 1 · Grammar range

Build flexible sentences

Use a mixture of simple, compound and complex sentences. The goal is variety with control, not complexity for its own sake.

01

Complex sentences

Join a main clause with a dependent clause to show reason, contrast, time or condition.

Although · contrastAlthough online learning is convenient, it may reduce face-to-face interaction.
Whereas · direct contrastUrban areas often have better public transport, whereas rural communities may rely on private vehicles.
While · contrast or timeWhile technology improves access to information, it can also expose users to misinformation.
Because · reasonMany employees prefer flexible working because it reduces commuting time.
Common mistake: Do not write “Although ..., but ...”. Use “Although ..., ...” or “..., but ...”.

Tip: Use the connector that expresses the exact relationship. Do not add connectors just to make a sentence look advanced.

02

Relative clauses

Add information about a person, thing or idea without starting a separate sentence.

Who · peopleTeachers who receive regular training are better prepared to use digital tools.
Which · things or ideasPublic transport, which is often cheaper than driving, can reduce household expenses.
That · defining informationPolicies that support renewable energy can reduce dependence on fossil fuels.
Common mistake: Do not use “that” in a non-defining clause between commas. Use “which” for things in that structure.

Tip: If the information is essential to identify the noun, it is usually defining. If it is extra information, use commas.

03

Conditional sentences

Use conditionals to explain possible results, hypothetical situations or consequences.

Zero · general truthIf people do not exercise regularly, their physical fitness may decline.
First · realistic possibilityIf governments invest in public transport, traffic congestion may decrease.
Second · hypothetical situationIf schools had more funding, they could provide better learning facilities.
Third · imagined pastIf the city had expanded its metro system earlier, fewer residents might have become dependent on cars.
Common mistake: In the standard first conditional, use present simple after “if”, not “will”.

Tip: Second conditionals are useful for discussing hypothetical policy choices, but keep the meaning realistic and clear.

04

Passive voice

Use passive voice when the action or result matters more than the person doing it.

ActiveThe government introduced new environmental regulations.
PassiveNew environmental regulations were introduced by the government.
Academic useMore funding should be allocated to public hospitals.
Common mistake: Passive = an appropriate form of “be” + past participle (V3). Check the tense.

Tip: Passive voice is useful in formal writing, but do not use it in every sentence. Active voice is often clearer.

05

Parallel structures

Keep items in a list or paired expression in the same grammatical form.

Incorrect: Schools should teach students to analyse information, communicating clearly and teamwork.
Correct: Schools should teach students to analyse information, communicate clearly and work in teams.
Not only ... but alsoPublic transport not only reduces congestion but also improves air quality.

Tip: When you write a list, check that every item follows the same pattern: noun + noun + noun, or verb + verb + verb.

06

Concession and contrast

Acknowledge an opposing point, then explain why your position still stands.

Despite + noun / -ingDespite its high initial cost, renewable energy can provide long-term savings.
Even though + clauseEven though online courses are flexible, some learners need face-to-face support.
AdmittedlyAdmittedly, private cars offer convenience; however, they contribute to congestion.
NeverthelessOnline learning has limitations. Nevertheless, it can make education more accessible.
Common mistake: “Despite” is followed by a noun or -ing form, not a full clause. Use “although/even though” before a clause.

Tip: Concession is most useful when it genuinely strengthens your argument, not when it adds an unrelated opposing idea.

Part 2 · Accuracy first

Fix the small errors that keep repeating

A wide grammar range helps only when your sentences are accurate. These details deserve daily attention.

07

Articles: a, an, the and zero article

Choose an article based on whether the noun is general or specific, and whether it is countable.

Incorrect / less accurateBetter
Government should invest in education.The government should invest in education. (a specific government)
Internet has changed our lives.The internet has changed our lives.
People need good education.People need a good education.
She gave me an advice.She gave me some advice.

Tip: “Advice”, “information”, “research” and “equipment” are generally uncountable. Do not add “a/an” or plural “-s” to them.

08

Subject–verb agreement

The verb must agree with the real subject, not a nearby noun.

IncorrectCorrect
People believes this.People believe this.
Technology have changed society.Technology has changed society.
The number of students are rising.The number of students is rising.
There is many reasons.There are many reasons.
Remember: “The number of + plural noun” takes a singular verb. “A number of + plural noun” takes a plural verb.
ExamplesThe number of people using public transport has increased. / A number of people use public transport daily.
09

Word forms

Check whether the sentence needs a noun, verb, adjective or adverb.

IncorrectCorrect
Technology is develop rapidly.Technology is developing rapidly.
This has a negative affect.This has a negative effect.
Education is importance.Education is important.
People can success.People can succeed.
It is benefit for society.It is beneficial to society.

Tip: Learn word families together: develop (verb), development (noun), developed/developing (adjective), rapidly (adverb).

10

Prepositions

Learn the whole phrase rather than translating a preposition from Indonesian.

ExpressionExample
depend onSuccess may depend on access to education.
contribute toCars contribute to air pollution.
responsible forGovernments are responsible for public services.
benefit fromStudents benefit from regular feedback.
access toEveryone should have access to healthcare.
impact onTechnology has an impact on communication.
result inHeavy traffic can result in delays.
result fromSome health problems result from inactivity.

Tip: Keep a personal list of phrases you get wrong and review it before each essay.

11

Sentence boundaries and punctuation

Make sure each sentence is complete and correctly joined to the next one.

Comma splice: Many people prefer online learning, it is convenient.
Correct: Many people prefer online learning because it is convenient.
Also correct: Many people prefer online learning. It is convenient.

Use a full stop to separate complete ideas. Use commas after introductory clauses and around non-essential information.

Tip: Read your essay aloud. If you run out of breath or lose the main point, the sentence may be too long.

12

Singular, plural and countability

Check noun endings and whether the noun can be counted.

CountableMany students · fewer cars · several solutions
UncountableMuch information · less pollution · a great deal of research
Incorrect: There are many informations available online.
Correct: There is a lot of information available online.

Tip: Use “fewer” with countable plural nouns and “less” with uncountable nouns.

Part 3 · Lexical resource

Use vocabulary naturally and precisely

Band 7–8 vocabulary is not a competition to use the rarest words. Choose accurate words and natural word combinations.

13

Collocations

Collocations are words that naturally go together.

Less natural / incorrectNatural collocation
Make a researchConduct research
Give an impactHave an impact
Do a decisionMake a decision
Big problemSerious problem
Fast developmentRapid development
Solve the trafficEase / reduce traffic congestion
Increase the knowledgeImprove / expand knowledge

Tip: Save vocabulary as a phrase with an example sentence, not as a single translated word.

14

Academic noun phrases

Use precise noun phrases to express an idea efficiently.

BasicPeople use technology. It helps them communicate.
More conciseThe widespread use of digital technology has improved communication.

Useful patterns:

  • The increasing use of...
  • The growing demand for...
  • The widespread adoption of...
  • The negative impact of...
  • The development of...
  • The lack of access to...

Tip: Use noun phrases when they make your meaning clearer. Do not turn every verb into a noun; too many heavy noun phrases can make writing difficult to read.

15

Precise vocabulary

Choose a word that describes the exact meaning.

GeneralMore precise when appropriate
Bad effectAdverse effect
HelpSupport / facilitate / enable
CauseLead to / result in / contribute to
Go upRise / increase / grow
Go downFall / decline / decrease
ImportantSignificant / essential / crucial

Tip: Synonyms are not always interchangeable. Check meaning, grammar and context before replacing a word.

16

Referencing and avoiding repetition

Refer back to an idea without repeating the same noun in every sentence.

RepetitiveStudents use digital resources. Students can access materials anywhere. Students can study independently.
ImprovedStudents use digital resources to access materials from almost anywhere. These learners can therefore study more independently.

Useful references: this approach, such measures, these individuals, this development, this trend, the former, the latter.

Tip: The reader must know exactly what “this”, “these” or “such” refers to. Avoid vague references.

Part 4 · Coherence and cohesion

Build a paragraph that actually develops an idea

A paragraph should not be a collection of opinions. Each sentence should move the same main idea forward.

The PEER structure

  1. Point: State the main idea.
  2. Explain: Explain why or how it happens.
  3. Example: Give a relevant example.
  4. Result: Explain the consequence or connect back to the argument.
Tip: The example should support the explanation. Avoid adding an example that introduces a completely new argument.

Example: a developed body paragraph

Point: One major benefit of public transportation is its ability to reduce traffic congestion.

Explain: When more people choose buses and trains instead of private vehicles, fewer cars occupy the roads.

Example: For example, cities with reliable metro systems can encourage commuters to leave their cars at home.

Result: As a result, public transport can make urban travel more efficient and reduce pressure on road networks.

Topic sentence: make the paragraph's purpose clear

Too vague: There are many things to consider about technology.
Clear: One significant advantage of technology is that it makes educational resources more accessible.
Too broad: Public transport is good for everyone in every way.
Focused: Investing in public transport can reduce the number of private vehicles on urban roads.
Part 5 · IELTS Academic Writing Task 2

Write a clear, well-developed argument

Task 2 requires at least 250 words. Aim for a clear answer, a consistent position and fully developed main ideas. Practise finishing within 40 minutes.

Practice question · Opinion essay

Some people believe that governments should spend more money on public transportation rather than building new roads. To what extent do you agree or disagree?

Type: Agree / disagreeSuggested time: 40 minutesMinimum: 250 words

Recommended essay structure

Introduction · 2–3 sentencesParaphrase the topic, then state your position clearly.
Body paragraph 1Present your strongest reason. Explain it and give a relevant example.
Body paragraph 2Present another distinct reason, or acknowledge the opposing view and respond to it.
Conclusion · 1–2 sentencesSummarise your main reasons and restate your position. Do not introduce a new argument.
Time guide: Spend about 5 minutes planning, 30 minutes writing and 5 minutes checking. This is a practice guide, not a fixed rule.

Band 8-style model answer

The following is a model for learning structure, development and language. Do not memorise it as a template for unrelated questions.

Introduction

Some argue that public funds should be directed towards improving public transportation rather than expanding road networks. I largely agree with this view because reliable mass transit can reduce congestion and provide more sustainable access to urban areas, although targeted road improvements remain necessary.

Body paragraph 1

The main reason to prioritise public transportation is its potential to reduce traffic congestion. When buses, trains and metro systems are reliable and affordable, commuters are more likely to use them instead of travelling by private car. This can reduce the number of vehicles on busy roads, particularly during peak hours. For example, a well-connected metro network allows large numbers of people to travel across a city without adding to road traffic. Consequently, investment in mass transit can improve journey times while making better use of limited urban space.

Body paragraph 2

Another important consideration is environmental sustainability. Private vehicles contribute to air pollution and greenhouse gas emissions, whereas public transport can move more passengers using less road space per person. Admittedly, building and operating rail systems requires substantial funding; nevertheless, the long-term benefits may outweigh the initial costs if the services are well planned and widely used. Governments should therefore focus on accessible routes, dependable schedules and affordable fares to encourage a lasting shift in travel behaviour.

Conclusion

Overall, I believe public transportation deserves greater investment than general road expansion because it can address congestion and environmental concerns at the same time. However, essential road maintenance and improvements should not be neglected.

Notice the techniques: clear position, focused topic sentences, explanations, a relevant example, concession, contrast, natural collocations and a conclusion that matches the introduction.

Task 2 question types to practise

  1. Opinion: To what extent do you agree or disagree?
  2. Discussion: Discuss both views and give your own opinion.
  3. Advantages / disadvantages: Do the advantages outweigh the disadvantages?
  4. Problem / solution: What problems does this cause, and what solutions can you suggest?
  5. Two-part question: Why is this happening? Is it a positive or negative development?

Tip: Underline every instruction in the question. If it asks two questions, answer both directly.

Part 6 · IELTS Academic Writing Task 1

Report the key features, not every number

Task 1 requires at least 150 words. Spend about 20 minutes. Select and compare the most important information, and include a clear overview.

Illustrative practice data. Values: car 60% → 45%; bus 25% → 30%; train 15% → 25%.

Task 1 structure

  1. Introduction: Paraphrase what the chart, table, map or process shows.
  2. Overview: Identify the most important overall trends, differences or stages.
  3. Body paragraph 1: Report and compare a logical group of key details.
  4. Body paragraph 2: Report the remaining important details and comparisons.
Important: Do not give your opinion, explain causes that are not shown, or write a conclusion like Task 2. The overview is essential.

Useful Task 1 language

Describing increases

increased steadily · rose sharply · climbed gradually · experienced a slight rise

Describing decreases

declined gradually · fell significantly · dropped sharply · saw a slight decrease

Comparing

higher than · lower than · considerably greater · almost twice as high as · similar to

Proportions and approximation

accounted for · represented · approximately · just over · slightly below · roughly

Tense tip: Use past simple for completed periods in the past. Use present simple for timeless descriptions or what a chart shows. Choose tense based on the time information.

Task 1 model answer

The bar chart compares the proportions of commuters who travelled by car, bus and train in a city in 2000 and 2020.

Overall, cars remained the most commonly used form of transport in both years, although their share declined. By contrast, the proportions of commuters travelling by bus and train increased, with train use showing the largest rise.

In 2000, 60% of commuters travelled by car, making it considerably more popular than the other two forms of transport. The figure for buses stood at 25%, while trains accounted for just 15% of journeys.

By 2020, the proportion travelling by car had fallen by 15 percentage points to 45%. Bus use rose slightly, from 25% to 30%, whereas the share of train passengers increased by 10 percentage points to 25%. As a result, the gap between car and train use narrowed substantially over the period.

Notice: The answer groups related data, highlights the overall pattern, uses comparisons and reports percentage-point changes accurately. A change from 60% to 45% is a fall of 15 percentage points, not 15%.

Academic Writing Task 1 · Example set A

Bar chart: transport use

Question: The chart shows the percentage of commuters using cars, buses and trains in a city in 2000 and 2020. Summarise the information by selecting and reporting the main features, and make comparisons where relevant.

Data: Car 60% → 45%; bus 25% → 30%; train 15% → 25%.

Band 7 · Clear, but less detailed

The bar chart compares the percentages of people who travelled to work by car, bus and train in a city in 2000 and 2020.

Overall, cars were the most popular means of transport in both years, although their use decreased. In contrast, the proportions of people using buses and trains increased, with train use showing the biggest change.

In 2000, 60% of commuters travelled by car, compared with 25% who used buses and 15% who travelled by train. This means that car use was much higher than the other two forms of transport.

By 2020, the figure for cars had fallen to 45%. Meanwhile, bus use increased to 30%, while the percentage of train users rose to 25%. Therefore, the difference between car and train use became smaller over the period.

Although the figures changed over time, the ranking of the three transport modes did not: cars remained first, followed by buses and then trains. The chart therefore suggests a gradual shift rather than a complete change in commuters' preferences.

Why this is around Band 7: It has a clear overview, selects the main figures and makes relevant comparisons. However, some comparisons are general, and the vocabulary and sentence patterns are relatively safe and repetitive.
Band 7.5 · More precise and developed

The bar chart illustrates changes in the proportions of commuters who used cars, buses and trains in a city between 2000 and 2020.

Overall, travelling by car remained the dominant choice, despite a noticeable decline. By comparison, both forms of public transport became more popular, and train use recorded the largest proportional increase.

In 2000, cars accounted for 60% of commuters, more than twice the figure for buses, at 25%, and four times that for trains, at 15%. Over the following two decades, the share of car users fell by 15 percentage points to 45%.

Bus travel experienced a modest rise of five percentage points, reaching 30% in 2020. The proportion travelling by train increased more substantially, from 15% to 25%. As a result, the gap between car and train use narrowed from 45 to 20 percentage points.

The rise in train use was proportionally greater than the increase in bus travel. However, cars still represented the largest single share in the final year, so the overall pattern remained centred on private transport.

Why this is around Band 7.5: The overview is more analytical, data comparisons are more specific, and phrases such as “accounted for”, “percentage points” and “the gap narrowed” are used naturally. The response still uses a fairly straightforward range of sentence structures.
Band 8 · Precise, selective and controlled

The bar chart compares the proportions of commuters travelling by car, bus and train in a city in 2000 and 2020.

Overall, the car remained the most widely used mode of transport, although its share contracted considerably. Conversely, public transport gained ground, with rail travel experiencing the more pronounced increase.

Cars accounted for three-fifths of commuters in 2000, whereas buses and trains represented 25% and 15%, respectively. By 2020, the proportion travelling by car had fallen by 15 percentage points to 45%, though it still exceeded the figure for either public transport option individually.

Over the same period, the share of bus users edged up by five percentage points, reaching 30%. Train travel saw a larger increase, rising by 10 points to 25%. Consequently, the gap between car and rail use narrowed substantially, from 45 percentage points in 2000 to 20 in 2020.

This shift is also reflected in the relative changes: rail use rose by two-thirds, while the bus figure increased by one-fifth. These proportional changes reinforce the contrast between the stronger growth in rail travel and the decline in car use.

Why this is around Band 8: It groups information efficiently, makes precise comparisons and uses a wider but natural range of vocabulary (“contracted”, “gained ground”, “edged up”). Sentence structures are varied and controlled without adding unnecessary detail.
Academic Writing Task 1 · Example set B

Line graph: internet use by age group

Question: The line graph shows the percentage of people aged 18–25 and 55–65 who used the internet in a country between 2005 and 2020. Summarise the information by selecting and reporting the main features, and make comparisons where relevant.

Illustrative data: Ages 18–25: 20% (2005), 35% (2010), 55% (2015), 75% (2020). Ages 55–65: 5%, 10%, 20%, 40%.

Band 7 · Accurate overview and key figures

The line graph shows the percentage of people in two age groups who used the internet in a country from 2005 to 2020.

Overall, internet use increased in both groups throughout the period. However, younger people were more likely to use the internet in every year shown, although the older group also experienced considerable growth.

Among people aged 18 to 25, the figure rose from 20% in 2005 to 35% in 2010. It then increased to 55% in 2015 and reached 75% by 2020.

A similar upward trend occurred among those aged 55 to 65, but the percentages were lower. Internet use started at just 5% and doubled to 10% in 2010. It then rose to 20% in 2015 before reaching 40% in the final year.

The difference was particularly noticeable at the beginning of the period, when internet use among younger adults was four times the rate recorded for the older group. By the final year, the younger group still led, but internet access had become more common among older people.

Band 7 feature: The response covers the main trends and includes relevant data. It is clear, although it mainly describes each group separately and offers limited direct comparison.
Band 7.5 · Better comparisons

The line graph illustrates the proportions of people aged 18–25 and 55–65 who used the internet in a country between 2005 and 2020.

Overall, internet use rose steadily in both age groups, while the younger group consistently recorded higher levels. Nevertheless, the increase among older adults was substantial, and the difference between the groups narrowed slightly towards the end.

For 18–25-year-olds, the proportion increased from 20% in 2005 to 35% five years later. It continued to climb, reaching 55% in 2015 and 75% in 2020. The older group followed the same upward pattern, although from a much lower starting point of 5%. Its figure doubled to 10% in 2010, then rose to 20% and 40% in the subsequent years.

By 2020, the gap between the two groups stood at 35 percentage points, compared with 15 points in 2005.

This pattern indicates that internet adoption was not limited to younger adults. Although the younger group maintained a clear lead, the older population increasingly participated in online activities during the period shown.

Band 7.5 feature: It compares the groups directly and calculates the changing gap. The organisation is more efficient, with a little more synthesis rather than only listing figures.
Band 8 · Strong synthesis and selection

The line graph compares internet usage among people aged 18–25 and 55–65 in a country over the 15-year period from 2005 to 2020.

Overall, the proportion of users rose continuously in both cohorts. Younger adults remained considerably more likely to go online, but uptake among the older group accelerated sufficiently to reduce the gap between the two age ranges.

Among 18–25-year-olds, internet use climbed from 20% in 2005 to 35% in 2010, before reaching 55% in 2015 and 75% at the end of the period. Although the older cohort started at only 5%, its rate doubled in the first five years and continued to rise, reaching 20% in 2015 and 40% by 2020.

Despite the older cohort's faster relative growth, the absolute gap widened from 15 percentage points in 2005 to 35 points in 2020.

The strongest relative growth occurred among the older users, whose proportion became eight times its initial level. By comparison, the figure for younger adults increased by 55 percentage points, confirming that growth was substantial in both groups.

Why this is around Band 8: The response synthesises the trends, distinguishes relative growth from the absolute gap, and avoids listing every figure mechanically. Its language is varied but remains precise.
Academic Writing Task 2 · Example set A

Opinion essay: working from home

Question: Some people believe that working from home is more beneficial than working in an office. To what extent do you agree or disagree?

Band 7 · Clear position, straightforward development

Working from home has become more common, and some people believe it offers greater benefits than working in an office. I mostly agree with this view because it saves commuting time and gives employees more flexibility, although office work is still useful for teamwork.

One major advantage of working from home is that employees do not need to travel to work every day. This can save several hours each week, especially for people who live far from their workplace. They can use this time for their families or personal activities. In addition, working from home allows people to organise their schedules more easily, which may improve their work-life balance.

However, working in an office can make communication easier. Employees can discuss problems directly and build stronger relationships with colleagues. This is particularly important for new workers who need guidance. Despite this benefit, many of these activities can also take place through video meetings and online communication tools.

In conclusion, I believe working from home is generally more beneficial because it provides flexibility and reduces commuting time. Nevertheless, a combination of home and office working may be the most suitable arrangement for some organisations.

Another practical benefit is that employees may have greater freedom to choose an environment that suits their working style. For tasks requiring concentration, a quieter home setting can reduce interruptions. This is not true for everyone, however, as some people find it difficult to separate professional and personal responsibilities when both take place in the same space.

Why this is around Band 7: The position is clear and both sides are considered. Ideas are relevant, but explanations are fairly general and some vocabulary and sentence patterns are repeated.
Band 7.5 · More developed reasoning

Remote working is increasingly common, leading some to argue that it is more beneficial than being based in an office. I largely agree because it can improve employees' work-life balance and reduce time spent commuting, although in-person collaboration remains valuable for certain tasks.

The main advantage of working from home is the flexibility it provides. Employees can often organise their working day around periods when they are most productive, while also having more time for family responsibilities. This is particularly beneficial for people with long commutes, as the time saved can be used for rest or other activities. As a result, remote work may improve both job satisfaction and overall well-being.

Another benefit is the reduction in commuting costs and travel-related stress. Workers spend less on transport and are not exposed to daily traffic delays. Admittedly, office environments can support spontaneous discussions and help new employees learn from experienced colleagues. However, these advantages do not necessarily require staff to be present every day; scheduled office days and online collaboration platforms can provide a reasonable balance.

Overall, I believe the benefits of remote work outweigh its limitations for many roles, provided that employers maintain effective communication and support their staff.

These advantages are likely to be most significant for jobs that can be completed independently using digital tools. Employers should also set clear expectations about availability and performance, since flexibility does not automatically lead to better results. Without suitable management, remote employees may feel isolated or struggle to switch off after working hours.

Why this is around Band 7.5: The argument is more fully explained, examples are relevant, and the counterargument is answered rather than simply mentioned. Vocabulary is more precise, with generally smooth progression.
Band 8 · Fully developed and controlled

The expansion of remote working has prompted debate about whether employees are better off working from home than in a conventional office. I largely agree that home-based work offers greater overall benefits, particularly through increased flexibility and reduced commuting, although its suitability depends on the nature of the job.

A key advantage is the greater control employees have over how they organise their time. By removing the daily journey to and from work, remote arrangements allow people to devote more time to family responsibilities, exercise or rest. This can be especially significant in large cities, where commuting may consume several hours each day. Provided that workloads are manageable, the resulting flexibility can improve work-life balance without necessarily reducing productivity.

Remote work can also lower costs for both employees and employers. Workers spend less on transport and meals outside the home, while businesses may need less office space. Admittedly, face-to-face contact can encourage informal learning and strengthen team relationships. Nevertheless, these benefits can often be preserved through planned in-person meetings, particularly when tasks require close cooperation. Roles involving specialist equipment or direct customer service, however, may be less suited to a fully remote model.

In conclusion, remote work is generally more advantageous because it offers greater flexibility and can reduce unnecessary costs. A hybrid arrangement is likely to be the most practical solution for organisations whose roles involve both independent work and regular collaboration.

This approach also recognises that productivity should be judged by the quality of work rather than by physical presence alone. If organisations establish measurable goals and provide appropriate equipment, employees can remain accountable while enjoying greater autonomy. The balance between flexibility and collaboration is therefore more important than adopting one arrangement for every role.

Why this is around Band 8: The response qualifies its position, develops each argument with consequences, recognises that job type matters and integrates the opposing view. Vocabulary is precise and varied, while complex sentences remain controlled.
Academic Writing Task 2 · Example set B

Discussion essay: university education

Question: Some people think universities should focus on preparing students for employment, while others believe they should provide a broader education. Discuss both views and give your own opinion.

Band 7 · Both views addressed

There is debate about whether universities should mainly prepare students for jobs or provide a wider education. Both approaches have advantages, but I believe universities should combine practical training with broader academic learning.

On the one hand, preparing students for employment is an important role of universities. Many students attend university because they want to gain the skills needed for a particular career. Courses that include practical projects, work placements and industry knowledge can help graduates become more confident when they enter the workplace. This may also make it easier for employers to find suitable workers.

On the other hand, a broad education helps students develop skills that are useful in many situations. Subjects outside a student's main area can improve critical thinking and help them understand different perspectives. These abilities are important because people may change careers several times during their lives.

In my opinion, universities should provide both types of learning. Students need job-related skills, but they should also learn how to think independently and adapt to change. This combination can prepare them for both their first job and their future careers.

However, a purely vocational approach may become outdated if it focuses too narrowly on the current requirements of a particular occupation. Students also need to understand the wider social and ethical implications of their work. For this reason, practical courses should include opportunities to discuss broader issues rather than concentrating exclusively on technical procedures.

This balance also allows institutions to respond to local labour-market needs without sacrificing the broader purpose of higher education.

Why this is around Band 7: Both views are covered and the writer gives a clear opinion. The ideas are relevant but could be explored in more depth, with more specific explanation of how broad learning benefits employment.
Band 7.5 · Clearer comparison and support

People disagree about whether universities should concentrate on preparing students for specific careers or offer a broader academic education. Although employment-related training is essential, I believe a university education should also develop transferable skills that remain useful throughout a person's working life.

Those who favour career-focused courses argue that higher education should respond to the needs of employers. Practical modules, internships and professional projects allow students to apply theoretical knowledge in realistic settings. For example, engineering students who complete industry placements may become familiar with workplace standards before graduating. This can make the transition into employment smoother for both graduates and employers.

However, a broader education has benefits that extend beyond a student's first job. Studying different subjects can strengthen critical thinking, communication and the ability to assess unfamiliar problems. Since industries and technologies change, graduates may need to move into roles that are not directly related to their original degree. Transferable skills can therefore help them adapt to changing career demands.

In my view, universities should integrate both approaches. Specialist knowledge helps graduates enter a profession, while broader learning enables them to respond to new challenges over time.

Furthermore, broader learning can improve students' ability to communicate with people from different backgrounds. This is useful in almost every profession, where employees are often required to explain complex ideas to colleagues or clients. A curriculum that combines specialist knowledge with these wider capabilities is therefore likely to produce more adaptable graduates.

This is particularly important in economies where new technologies can quickly change the skills required in established professions.

Why this is around Band 7.5: The two positions are compared more directly and examples are better connected to the claims. The opinion is consistent, and the conclusion follows logically from the discussion.
Band 8 · Nuanced and well-integrated argument

There is ongoing debate over whether universities should prioritise professional preparation or provide students with a broad education. While close links with the labour market are important, I would argue that higher education should combine specialist training with the development of transferable intellectual skills.

Supporters of a career-oriented approach reasonably point out that graduates need skills that employers can use immediately. Courses designed with industry input, particularly those involving placements and practical projects, can narrow the gap between academic study and workplace expectations. In fields such as engineering or healthcare, supervised practice is especially important because theoretical understanding alone may not prepare students for professional responsibilities.

Nevertheless, defining university education solely in terms of immediate employability would be too narrow. A broad curriculum can cultivate critical thinking, clear communication and the ability to evaluate evidence. These capabilities are valuable not only in a first job but also when workers face technological change or move between occupations. A graduate who can learn independently may remain adaptable even when the specific tools or knowledge required by an industry evolve.

In my view, the two purposes are complementary rather than competing. Universities should provide rigorous subject-specific training while ensuring that students can analyse unfamiliar problems, communicate effectively and continue learning beyond graduation.

Such a model would not mean that every student must study a large number of unrelated subjects. Instead, universities could build communication, ethical reasoning and problem-solving into specialist courses. In this way, broad education would strengthen professional preparation rather than compete with it.

Why this is around Band 8: The writer examines the assumptions behind both views, uses a relevant field-specific example, and reaches a clear synthesis. The argument is nuanced but still easy to follow; vocabulary and grammar are flexible without sounding forced.

Part 7 · Avoid score-limiting habits

Common Band 7–8 traps

1. Overcomplicated sentences

Long sentences become hard to control. Split an idea when clarity suffers.

2. Memorised phrases

A memorised expression can sound unnatural or fail to answer the question.

3. Unnatural vocabulary

A precise common word is better than an impressive word used incorrectly.

4. Repeated examples

Develop different reasons instead of repeating one point in new words.

5. Unclear position

State your opinion in the introduction and keep it consistent.

6. Missing Task 1 overview

Do not describe every figure without summarising the main features.
